## 1. Introduction (~200–250 words)

**Opening move:** one or two sentences establishing why this transition is happening and why it matters right now. Concrete, not "AI is transforming the world."

**The problem:** what is unclear, contested, or puzzling about it? This is what your RQ resolves.

**Research question:**
> *How does [theoretical concept] explain [outcome/phenomenon] in [bounded case]?*

**Theoretical angle:** name the concept and its source (e.g. "I apply the functional approach to Technological Innovation Systems (Bergek et al., 2008) to identify system weaknesses that slow adoption in…").

**Scope statement:** one sentence bounding the case — sector, geography, timeframe.

### Drafting prompts
- What exactly is transitioning, from what to what?
- Who are the actors?
- Why hasn't this been answered already?
- Would a classmate need to ask a follow-up question to understand your scope? Fix that now.

## 2. Methods (~200–250 words)

**Why this case.** Why is this a good case for this concept? (Revelatory, typical, extreme, or longitudinal are defensible reasons.)

**Why this theory.** What does this lens let you see that a different one wouldn't?

**Empirical data — structured.** 4–5 newspaper articles from SvD, DN and DI, accessed via Mediearkivet. Note the date range and how you selected them.

**Empirical data — unstructured.** Company sites, blogs, press releases. Be honest about the balance: press gives you framing, company material gives you claims.

**Journal articles.** 4–5 at CABS 2+, chosen at the intersection of concept and case. Note the search strategy.

**Guest lectures.** Which one(s), and what specifically they contributed.

**Limitations.** One or two sentences. Press coverage is mediated and reactive; a single case limits generalisability. Naming these reads as competence, not weakness.

### Drafting prompts
- Could a reader reproduce your evidence search?
- Is there a mismatch between ambition and evidence?

## 3. Findings (~400–550 words)

Organise by **analytical point**, not by source. Each paragraph should advance the answer to your RQ.

**Paragraph shape that works:**
> **Claim** → **evidence** (newspaper / journal / guest lecture) → **what it means for the RQ**

**Use all three evidence types, visibly:**
- Newspaper: *"Dagens Industri reported that… (Author, 2025)"*
- Journal: theoretical framing or comparison case
- **Guest lecture** — explicitly required: *"In his guest lecture on 17 November, John Diklev of Flower described…"*

**Things that earn credit:**
- Tension or contradiction between sources — don't smooth it over, analyse it
- Direct quotes from the guest lecture
- Using the theory's actual vocabulary (TIS functions, regime/niche, adopter categories), not generic words

**Things that lose credit:**
- Serial summary of articles with no argument
- Listing sources instead of using them
- No mention of the RQ in this section

## 4. Reflections on Societal Challenges / Sustainability (~250–350 words)

**Required:** a *critical* reflection on how the findings relate to relevant societal challenges or sustainability issues.

**Move beyond "this is good for the environment."** Useful angles:
- **Distributional:** who gains, who loses, which regions/workers/sectors?
- **Burden-shifting** (Lai & Karakaya, 2024): has the problem moved rather than been solved? E.g. electrification shifting emissions upstream.
- **Rebound effects:** efficiency gains that increase total consumption.
- **Lock-in and path dependency:** does this transition foreclose alternatives?
- **Legitimacy and inclusion:** whose voice is absent from the transition?
- **Reflexivity** (Susur & Karakaya, 2021): are the assumptions behind the transition itself questionable?

Tie back to **your** findings. Generic sustainability commentary doesn't count.
